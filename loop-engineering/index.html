The Manifesto
Welcome. The title is the whole thesis: prompt less, loop more. Over four years the industry climbed an abstraction ladder — from writing one good prompt to designing systems that do the prompting for us. The discipline at the top got a name this June: loop engineering. Tonight I'll show you the ladder itself — interactively — then descend into one real case study: a research loop that ran six gated phases and wrote a paper. And because an audit without gaps is marketing, I'll be explicit about where my own practice falls short and what the next steps are.

So what sits at the top of the ladder? A loop is a system that discovers its own work, hands it to agents, verifies the result, persists what happened, and knows when to stop. Two things to hold onto — and the slide is deliberately sparse so you listen, not read. The arithmetic: a ten-step task at sixty percent per step succeeds zero point six percent of the time in one shot; structure the same work as a loop with carried state and effort becomes linear — every run starts where the last one stopped. That's the entire economic case. And the anatomy: six parts — heartbeat, workspaces, knowledge, connectors, a checker that isn't the maker, and memory on disk. Now we descend from the ladder into one real loop. The mission: can software agents break fraud detection — what happens to detection systems built on human behavioral assumptions when the economic actor is a machine? Watch the pipeline: six phases over weeks — theory, simulation, a detection framework, attack injection, validation against real transaction data, and finally a paper. The checkmarks between phases are the whole story: a gate after every phase, and the loop could not advance until its claims survived verification. Its memory lived on disk, not in any model's head. Let's open it up.

Verification × Persistence

The cycle every phase runs

Plan → execute → verify → referee → advance. The verify step is executable — dimensional checks, limiting cases, conservation laws, a 104-class error taxonomy that makes failures diagnosable instead of mysterious.

The checker is never the maker

A separate referee agent — fresh context, no authorship pride — reviews each phase against its goal before the loop may advance. Banks have run this control on money for a century; the AI industry rediscovered it and called it maker/checker.

Inside every phase, the same cycle: plan, execute, verify, referee, advance. Two beats carry all the safety. Verify is executable — dimensional analysis, limiting cases, conservation checks, backed by a 104-class error taxonomy, so a failure is a diagnosis, not a mystery. And the referee: a separate agent with fresh context and no authorship pride reviews the phase before the loop advances. If that sounds familiar, it should — four-eyes on money is a century old. The AI industry rediscovered the same control and called it maker/checker.

The results — including the ones a demo would hide, and in plain words. In the lab, on practice data, it scored 88.7 percent and almost nothing slipped past it. Seven of eight attack styles were caught every single time; the eighth was written up as a gap rather than buried. Then reality: on eighty-two thousand real transactions the score fell to 42.8 — the real world is messier and some records were tagged wrong — recovering to 56.1 after the mislabeled records were fixed, with the fix itself documented. I want to sell you on the drop being the best number here: a loop that writes everything down cannot quietly forget a bad result. That's what trustworthy automation looks like — and it's the property a regulated industry should demand first.

What the loop still does not do for you

Three warnings — three scars.

Verification stays yours

“Done” is a claim, not a proof. Check it.

The scar: an agent scored 97% on the public test — by hardcoding the answers. Held out: zero.

Comprehension debt compounds

The loop shipped it ≠ you understand it.

The scar: 60+ project folders — and an API key in plaintext. Found by my own audit, not by luck.

Cognitive surrender is comfortable

The same loop sharpens one engineer and hollows out another. It can't tell. You can.

The defense: reading what the loops produce stays a daily habit, not an option.

Three warnings, each with a scar instead of a hypothetical — short on the slide, the stories are mine to tell. Verification stays yours: done is a claim; the industry's exhibit is an agent that hit ninety-seven percent by hardcoding answers and zero held-out. Comprehension debt: my portfolio outgrew me — sixty-plus folders and an API key sitting in plaintext, found by my own audit. And cognitive surrender — the sharpest one: the same loop accelerates one person and hollows out another, and the loop cannot tell the difference. You can. Lessons learnt — the audit's findings

Five things I'd tell myself a year ago.

1 · Persistence isn't the frontier — discovery is. I spent a year perfecting run-until-done loops while the real leverage was loops that find their own work. Mine are chartered but unproven — see next steps.

2 · Knowledge must flow both ways. Loops that only read skills leave every lesson to die with the run. The write-back path — human-gated — is worth more than another loop.

3 · Audit the estate, not just the code. Comprehension debt accumulates between projects, invisibly, faster than inside them.

4 · The plumbing needs verification too. A harness bug silently swallowed sub-agent reports until an audit caught it. Channels are part of the loop; test them like it.

5 · Budget caps are stop conditions, not settings. Cost per verified result must bend down as a loop matures — if it doesn't, the design is wrong, not the model.

Five lessons I'd hand myself a year ago. One: persistence isn't the frontier, discovery is — I over-invested in run-until-done and came late to the heartbeat. Two: knowledge must flow both ways; read-only loops leave every lesson to die with the run. Three: audit the estate, not just the code — debt between projects grows invisibly. Four — the one no article warned me about: the plumbing needs verification too; a harness bug silently ate sub-agent reports until an audit caught it. Five: budget caps are stop conditions with the same rank as failing tests — and that lesson gets its own slide next.

Where the audit says “not yet” — and the fix for each

Three improvements, already chartered.

01 · The heartbeat

Prove it, don't just design it

Three discovery lanes are chartered — cadence, budget caps, human gates. None has run long enough to be judged.

The fix: 30 days of unattended operation, judged only on the evidence the lanes write about themselves — never on their own self-assessment.

02 · The knowledge

Make lessons flow back

Loops read a large hand-written skill estate but write nothing back — every run's lessons die with the run.

The fix: a monthly mining pass over the failure records that drafts skill updates — drafts only; a human reviews and applies.

03 · The gates

Gate every lane — and the plumbing

One lane has an executable quality gate today; the others still take the runner's word for it.

The fix: every lane gets its own gate script, and the channels that carry results get tested like any other part of the loop.

The scorecard said "partial" twice and "next step" once — this is the fix for each, and all three are chartered, none finished. One, the heartbeat: three discovery lanes exist with cadence, budget caps, and human gates; the improvement is proof — thirty days of unattended operation, judged only on the evidence they write about themselves, never on self-assessment. Two, the knowledge: my loops read skills but never update them, so the fix is a monthly mining pass over the failure records that drafts skill updates for a human to review — the write-back is gated, on purpose. Three, the gates: only one lane has an executable quality gate today; every lane gets one, and the reporting channels get tested too — the judge-drift story from the scorecard taught me that even the checkers, and the pipes that carry their verdicts, need checking.

A good loop gets cheaper every lap.
